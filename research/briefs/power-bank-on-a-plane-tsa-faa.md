## Topic

What TSA and the FAA say about carrying a power bank on a US flight: carry-on only, the 100 Wh size limit, how to work out watt hours, and why the airline's own rules still matter.

## Working title (proposal)

Can You Bring a Power Bank on a Plane? What TSA and the FAA Say

## Search intent and focus keyword

- **Proposed focus keyword:** `can you bring a power bank on a plane`
- **Intent:** informational and rule-checking, a pre-trip question.
- **Demand signal:** **not measured.** The autocomplete tool was unreliable on 2026-10-07 and the search-engine pages could not be opened in the pane. ## Audience

US travelers packing a phone charger or power bank for a domestic flight.

## Angle

1. **Where it goes (TSA).** "Portable chargers or power banks containing a lithium ion battery must be packed in carry-on bags. Spare lithium batteries, which include both power banks and phone chargers, are prohibited in checked luggage." The page's header shows "Carry On Bags: Yes; Checked Bags: No".
2. **How big (FAA, TSA).** FAA table: spare battery or power bank "limited to a rating of 100 watt hours (Wh) per battery. With airline approval, passengers may also carry up to two spare larger lithium-ion batteries (101-160 Wh)." FAA FAQ: batteries "exceeding 160 Wh are forbidden". TSA's over-100 Wh page gives the same two-battery, 101–160 Wh, airline-approval rule.
3. **How to find the number (FAA).** "A lithium-ion battery's Wh should be marked on the battery; if not, you will need to determine the Wh." Volts x amp hours; for mAh divide by 1000 first. FAA's example: a 12-volt, 8 Ah battery is 96 Wh.
4. **How many (FAA).** "There are no limits for rechargeable batteries under 100 Wh, provided they are for personal use by the passenger." and "Many airlines ... may have stricter quantity and Wh limits ... regardless of Wh capacity. Please check with your air carrier".
5. **The officer decides (TSA).** Each TSA page ends: "The final decision rests with the TSA officer on whether an item is allowed through the checkpoint."

## Scope: in and out

- **In:** the five points above, each attributed to TSA or the FAA with the as-of date; one sentence that the airline's own limits can be stricter.
- **Out:** use of power banks during the flight, any airline's specific policy, international rules, e-cigarettes, wheelchairs and mobility devices, lithium metal batteries, devices in checked bags, brand or product recommendations, battery-fire explanation beyond one attributed sentence, safety advice of Claude's own.
- The article describes what the agencies say; it gives no personal advice.

## Official source notes (read 2026-10-07 in the built-in browser)

- **TSA, "Power Banks"**, https://www.tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/whatcanibring/items/power-banks . "Carry On Bags: Yes. Checked Bags: No." "Portable chargers or power banks containing a lithium ion battery must be packed in carry-on bags. Spare lithium batteries, which include both power banks and phone chargers, are prohibited in checked luggage." "For more information, see the FAA guidance on portable rechargers." "Last Updated on May 14, 2025". "The final decision rests with the TSA officer on whether an item is allowed through the checkpoint."
- **TSA, "Lithium batteries with more than 100 watt hours"**, https://www.tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/whatcanibring/items/lithium-batteries-more-100-watt-hours . "Carry On Bags: Yes (Special Instructions). Checked Bags: No." "Spare (uninstalled) lithium ion and lithium metal batteries, including power banks and cell phone battery charging cases, must be carried in carry-on baggage only." "With airline approval, passengers may also carry up to two spare larger lithium ion batteries (101–160 Wh) or lithium metal batteries (2-8 grams)." "There is a limit of two spare batteries per person for the larger lithium ion batteries described above (101–160 watt hours per battery." "Last Updated on March 29, 2023".
- **FAA PackSafe, "Airline Passengers and Batteries"**, https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/airline-passengers-and-batteries . "Last updated: Friday, October 2, 2026". "Based on US DOT regulations (49 CFR, Sec. 175.10). Transportation Security Administration (TSA) security, individual airline, and international rules may, at times, be more restrictive." Table row "Spare Battery or Power Bank": "Lithium-ion batteries are limited to a rating of 100 watt hours (Wh) per battery. With airline approval, passengers may also carry up to two spare larger lithium-ion batteries (101-160 Wh). Must be carry-on only and protected from damage and short circuit. Many airlines, both domestic and international, may have stricter quantity and Wh limits to include how many power banks/portable chargers each passenger is allowed to travel with, regardless of Wh capacity. Please check with your air carrier for its specific restrictions before traveling." Carry-on YES, checked NO. FAQ: "Rechargeable batteries with a capacity of 0-100 Wh are allowed on passenger aircraft, while those with a capacity of 101-160 Wh require approval from the air carrier, and batteries exceeding 160 Wh are forbidden." "There are no limits for rechargeable batteries under 100 Wh, provided they are for personal use by the passenger. Batteries carried for further sale or distribution (vendor samples, etc.) are prohibited." Watt-hour formula and the 12 V x 8 Ah = 96 Wh example. "Damaged or recalled batteries ... must not be carried aboard an aircraft ... unless the damaged or recalled battery has been removed or otherwise made safe." Flight crew guidance: "Passengers should notify the flight crew immediately if their lithium-ion battery or portable electronic device is overheating, expanding, smoking, or burning." (FAQ answers were collapsed on the page and read from the page text.)

## Risks

- The TSA "Power Banks" page does not state the 100 Wh number; it comes from the TSA over-100 Wh page and the FAA table. The article must attribute each number to the page that states it.
- Airlines' own power-bank rules (including in-flight use) were not researched and may be stricter than the agencies' rules; the article must not imply the agency rules are the whole story.
- A safety topic: no advice beyond what the agencies state, and no claim about how likely fires are.
- The FAA is not TSA; the title names both and the text says which agency says what.
- Search demand and competition are unknown.
