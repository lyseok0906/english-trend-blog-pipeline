# USPS Money Orders: How to Buy, Cash and Replace One

**USPS pages describe a postal money order as a document similar to U.S. currency that you buy at a Post Office, send to a payee, and that the payee can cash at a Post Office or many banks.** The pages say it does not expire, pays only the exact amount printed on it and cannot be stopped once issued, so the receipt you keep is what you rely on if it is lost, stolen or damaged. This article walks through buying, cashing, checking and replacing one, and shows where the pages word things differently.

This article uses the USPS FAQ "Money Orders - The Basics" (Article 000006987, dated June 10, 2026), the USPS.com page "Sending Money Orders" (no update date shown) and section 3.0 of Domestic Mail Manual (DMM) 509, "Other Services" (no update date shown), all read on November 26, 2026. The pages state fees, a maximum amount for a single domestic money order and amounts at which extra paperwork applies; this article gives none of those figures.

## Buying a money order

The FAQ says domestic money orders can be bought at any Post Office, from rural carriers and at some contract stations, and that military money orders must be bought at an APO, FPO or DPO facility. The DMM adds that rural and authorized highway contract route carriers sell them: the customer fills out Form 6387, hands it and the payment to the carrier, and the carrier delivers the money order on the next trip unless the customer pays for mailing.

For payment, the FAQ lists U.S. currency and coins and ATM or debit cards at locations where the customer's PIN is entered on a keypad. It says checks are not accepted, credit cards may not be used and traveler's checks are no longer accepted. USPS.com says to take cash or a debit card and that you cannot pay with a credit card.

The DMM says the purchaser completes the money order and the customer's receipt with the names and addresses of the payee and the purchaser, and, in section 3.2.4 on how the purchaser completes it, that a money order must be made payable to a single identifiable party, which can be the purchaser. Section 3.3.5, on cashing, separately covers a money order completed with more than one payee; see "Cashing one" below. The FAQ says several money orders can be bought in one visit with no daily limit on how many, but that large daily totals trigger a Funds Transaction Report and acceptable ID; this article does not give the amounts. The FAQ's headline notice says that since October 1, 2025, USPS has stopped cashing all international money orders.

## Cashing one

USPS.com says you can cash a postal money order at a Post Office for free and can also cash it at most banks and some stores, and that rural carriers may cash them if they have enough money on hand. The FAQ says cashing at a Post Office depends on the funds available at that location, and that a question about whether a particular bank will cash one has to go to that bank.

The FAQ and USPS.com give the same steps: do not sign the money order before cashing, bring identification, and sign it at the counter in front of a USPS employee. The FAQ says an acceptable primary form of identification is required; USPS.com says to take a primary photo ID. The DMM says customers must sign in the presence of a USPS employee, that acceptable ID can be required, and that USPS may refuse payment if identity is not proved to the employee's satisfaction. It also sets rules about who may be paid. Section 3.3.5 covers a money order that was completed with more than one payee: it is paid to either payee if the conjunction "or" connects them, and all listed payees must endorse it if "and" or no conjunction is used. The DMM does not say how this fits with the single-party wording of section 3.2.4, which is about how the purchaser completes the order. A money order payable to an organization must be signed in its name by an authorized representative, and a person who cashes it for someone else needs, for example, a power of attorney filed at the Post Office. The DMM says domestic money orders are paid regardless of age and are not interest-bearing, and the FAQ says they never expire.

## Checking the status

USPS.com says you can check the status of a money order you bought from USPS in the Money Orders Application, using the serial number, the Post Office number and the dollar amount. The FAQ says near real-time status needs those three items from the receipt, and that the status shows whether it has been cashed. It also says you can identify which Post Office facility sold a given money order, but you cannot stop payment on a money order or track it in the mail.

## If it is lost, stolen or damaged

The FAQ prints the wording on the back of the customer's receipt: it is your guarantee for a refund if the money order is lost or stolen, provided you filled in the Pay To and From information, and no claim for improper payment is permitted one year after payment. The DMM adds that USPS does not guarantee a full refund on a stolen money order if the receipt is not presented with the claim.

For a lost or stolen one, the FAQ and USPS.com say USPS replaces it only if it has not been cashed, and that there are no stop payments. You take the receipt to any Post Office, start a Money Order Inquiry at the counter and pay an inquiry fee, whose amount this article does not give. You can then check the status of the inquiry in the Money Orders Application, and a confirmed loss that was not cashed gets a replacement. The FAQ says loss or theft can take up to 30 days to confirm and up to 60 days to investigate. For defective, damaged or spoiled money orders, the DMM and the FAQ say USPS replaces them without charge if you return the money order and the matching receipt; the FAQ says it is reissued immediately and you do not receive a refund. For a damaged or spoiled one, the FAQ adds that if you do not have the original receipt you may still complete the inquiry form with all of the required information and return it to the window clerk. It states this in its steps for damaged or spoiled money orders and does not repeat it in its lost or stolen steps.

To get a copy of a cashed money order, the FAQ says to file an inquiry with the receipt, that it may take up to 15 days for a domestic money order, and that a photocopy is only available for 2 years from the date it was cashed. This inquiry is separate from a claim for mail or a package, which our article [USPS Claims: Filing Periods, Who Can File and What You Need](https://statesideexplained.com/usps-claim-how-it-works/) covers.

## Spotting a fake

USPS.com says to check four things on a money order you are given: that the dollar amount is not more than the domestic maximum and matches in words and numbers, that there is no discoloration or disturbed paper fiber around the amount, that the watermarks are not easy to see without holding the paper up to light, and that the design is one of the valid ones. The FAQ says to hold the money order up to a light and look for the watermarks and the security thread, and that the newest design has a QR code that leads to USPS's status check. To verify one, USPS.com lists scanning the QR code, using the Money Orders tool on USPS.com or calling the Money Order Verification System. If you suspect fraud, both pages say to contact the U.S. Postal Inspection Service.

## Where the USPS pages differ

- **Traveler's checks.** The FAQ says traveler's checks are no longer accepted to buy a money order and USPS.com lists only cash or a debit card. The DMM still lists established traveler's checks payable in U.S. dollars if the purchase is for at least half of their value.
- **When a replacement or refund comes.** The FAQ says that if a money order has not been cashed within the first 30 days after the sale, a customer can receive a refund by USPS check, and elsewhere that a refund may come if it is not cashed within 10 business days after the inquiry is received. The receipt wording the FAQ prints says a replacement is not issued until 60 days after the purchase date. The pages do not reconcile these.
- **One payee or several.** DMM 3.2.4, on completing the money order, says it must be made payable to a single identifiable party. DMM 3.3.5, on cashing, sets rules for a money order completed with more than one payee. They address different steps, and the DMM does not reconcile them.
- **Where to buy.** The FAQ names rural carriers and some contract stations, USPS.com names Post Office locations, and the DMM names Post Offices, branches, stations, military facilities and rural and highway contract route carriers.

## What the pages do not say

They do not say how long a refund check takes to arrive, or whether any particular bank or store will cash a postal money order. The fees, the maximum for a single domestic money order and the daily totals that need extra paperwork are stated on the pages, but this article leaves out those amounts. DMM 509 sections other than 3.0 were not read.
