# USPS Change of Address: How to File, Who Can File and What Happens Next

**USPS pages describe a change of address (COA) as a request to reroute your mail, including letters and packages, to a new address, which you file online or at a Post Office after your identity is verified.** The FAQ says a request can be submitted 90 days before the effective date of your move and up to 30 days after it, and that mail will only be forwarded if you submit one. USPS.com adds that a change of address only changes your mailing address with the Post Office, so you still have to tell senders yourself.

This article uses the USPS FAQ "Change of Address - The Basics" (Article 000005313, dated October 15, 2026) and the USPS.com page "Standard Forward Mail & Change of Address" (no update date shown), both read on November 26, 2026. It gives no fees or prices. How long mail is forwarded for each mail class is in our article [How Long Does USPS Mail Forwarding Last?](https://statesideexplained.com/usps-mail-forwarding-how-long/), so this one covers the filing side.

## Online or at a Post Office

| | Online | At a Post Office |
|---|---|---|
| How you start | Choose "Individual," "Family" (everyone has the same last name) or "Business" and complete the form | Ask for a free Mover's Guide packet and fill in PS Form 3575 from it |
| How identity is checked | Opt in to a verification code or link on your mobile phone and pay an identity verification fee; the card's billing address must match your old or new address | The retail associate uses your photo ID to verify your identity |
| What you bring | A card; USPS.com says that if online verification succeeds, a confirmation code is emailed | Acceptable photo ID and any other required documents |
| Notes | The FAQ says the fee lets USPS check the card with the issuing bank | The FAQ says PS Form 3575 can no longer be printed from a home computer |

USPS.com says a request must be made in person at a Post Office if you are moving outside the United States, are filing for someone else, or cannot verify your identity online. For a move to or from a military address, the FAQ says the request must be completed online and that PS Form 3575 cannot be used. The FAQ also says that if you mail or submit the paper form without proper authentication at a Post Office, you will receive a letter telling you to finish authentication online or in person. If you cannot verify your identity at a Post Office either, USPS.com says you cannot complete a change of address with USPS and your only option is to contact everyone who might send you mail yourself.

## Which move type to choose

The FAQ ties the choice to who is moving. One person moving files an Individual move. An entire family with the same last name moving to the same address files one Family move. People with different last names, or only some members of a household, file separate Individual requests. A business files one Business move, and the FAQ says up to 50 business alias names can go in one request when the old and new addresses are the same for all of them.

The FAQ lists several cases it does not allow. A change of address cannot be filed for an individual's mail addressed to an organization or to the person at a place of employment; the organization can redirect that mail itself. An individual or family cannot file one from a Commercial Mail Receiving Agency (CMRA) address, although a request to a CMRA address is allowed. Individuals and families moving to a foreign country cannot file online. The FAQ also says USPS does not offer mail forwarding to people who live and travel in recreational vehicles.

## Filing for someone else

Both pages say an authorized agent goes in person to a Post Office with their own valid photo ID. The FAQ lists the documents: for a minor child, a birth certificate or a legal document showing custody or guardianship; for a person unable to act for themselves, a Power of Attorney or guardianship papers; for a deceased person, executor documents or a court-appointed representative; for yourself under a different last name, a marriage certificate or divorce documents. For a business filing the paper form, the FAQ says USPS requires a business license or a signed letter on company letterhead from someone in a leadership role. The FAQ and USPS.com both say a death certificate alone is not enough for a deceased person.

## What you receive and when mail moves

The FAQ says USPS promptly mails a Move Validation Letter to the address you are leaving. Within 5 postal business days before the start date, a Welcome Kit with a Customer Notification Letter arrives at the new address. The letter contains a 16-digit Confirmation Code, which the FAQ says you need to change or cancel the request without visiting a Post Office. Online filers also get the code by email.

On the start date, the FAQ says USPS stops delivering to the old address, and mail starts arriving at the new one within 7-10 postal business days of the start date. During that period the mail is inaccessible: the FAQ says you cannot pick it up at the old Post Office or a processing facility, and there is no estimated delivery time for forwarded mail. It also says mail will not be forwarded if the mailer told USPS to return undeliverable mail, and USPS.com says government agencies and financial institutions do not allow forwarding of sensitive documents such as identity documents and bank statements. If a change of address matches your Informed Delivery account, the FAQ says that account's access can be suspended for security; our article [What Is USPS Informed Delivery? How the Free Preview Works](https://statesideexplained.com/usps-informed-delivery-how-it-works/) covers that service.

## Changing, cancelling or disputing

To modify or cancel online, the FAQ says you need your new ZIP Code and the Confirmation Code, at the Manage My Move site. If you lose the code, you visit a Post Office. The FAQ sets these limits: no more than two changes in a single day, one of which can involve the street address, and a cancellation is still allowed after that. A cancellation takes 72 hours to take effect. A Family move can be switched to Individual but not the reverse, and a Temporary move can be switched to Permanent but not the reverse. Changes to the address used in identity validation are allowed only if the new address has the same ZIP Code.

If you receive a Change of Address validation letter but are not moving, the FAQ says you can file a dispute online using the 13-digit key and ZIP Code printed on the letter, and USPS Support will investigate.

## If you do not file one

The FAQ says that if you move without filing, your carrier picks up accumulated mail and leaves a notice that it is available for pickup. The local Post Office holds accumulated mail for up to 10 calendar days, after which most items are returned to the sender and items that cannot be forwarded or returned are discarded. Hold Mail is a separate service; see [How Does USPS Hold Mail Work? Limits and Steps](https://statesideexplained.com/usps-hold-mail-how-it-works/).

## Two timing details the pages word differently

- **The 7-10 business days.** USPS.com says that once your request is approved, you should allow at least 7-10 business days for it to go into effect. The FAQ says mail starts arriving at your new address within 7-10 postal business days of the start date. The first describes how long a change takes to take effect after approval; the second describes how long mail takes to arrive after the start date. The pages do not link the two, so this article does not add them together.
- **Temporary forwarding periods.** The FAQ says the minimum for temporary forwarding is 15 days and that a temporary request can forward mail for a maximum of 364 days from the start date. It also describes a first request of up to 185 days, which a new request can extend by up to 179 more days, with a total forwarding time that cannot exceed 12 months. The FAQ adds that if you return to the original address for at least 45 days, the 364-day limit starts over. USPS.com describes a temporary request as for relocating for 15 days up to 1 year. The pages do not present these as conflicting: the FAQ spells out the total and how an extension fits into it, and USPS.com gives the general range.

## What the pages do not say

They do not say how long approval takes. They do not list which photo IDs are accepted; this article did not read USPS's page on that. They give the identity verification fee and other fee amounts, which this article leaves out. The FAQ points to Domestic Mail Manual section 507.2.0 for forwarding exceptions and mail classes it does not list; that section was not read.
